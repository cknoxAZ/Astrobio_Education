Teaching Approaches / Place-based learning

Designing Place-Based Astrobiology Education

Place needs to be integral to how scientific questions are developed, investigated, and interpreted.

A useful instructional model would involve five interconnected components.

1. Begin with a meaningful place or phenomenon

Learners identify a local environment, observation, or environmental issue that can generate questions about life and habitability. The starting point might be a desert landscape, pond, neighborhood, soil sample, night sky, or seasonal environmental change.

2. Investigate the relationships within that place

Learners gather observations and evidence, examine physical and biological processes, and consider how organisms interact with their environments. They may also investigate the historical and human dimensions of the place when relevant to the question.

3. Connect local findings to astrobiological questions

Learners compare their observations with scientific evidence from other planetary environments. They explore questions concerning habitability, adaptation, biosignatures, planetary evolution, or the origins of life.

4. Examine the limitations and assumptions of the comparison

Learners consider what their local evidence can and cannot tell them about another world. They identify uncertainties, alternative explanations, and assumptions about the nature of life.

5. Return to place through reflection and communication

Learners reconsider their original environment in light of what they have learned. They communicate their findings, explain the connections between local and planetary environments, and reflect on the scientific, cultural, or ethical implications of their inquiry.

This progression is not intended as a rigid sequence. Rather, it illustrates how learning can move repeatedly between local observations and broader questions, with each informing the other.

For example, students investigating a saline lake might examine how salinity, pH, and mineral composition affect habitability. They could use those observations to consider possible environments on early Earth or Mars, while examining the limitations of the analogy. They might also investigate the lake's ecological significance, human history, and contemporary environmental conditions.

Similarly, students could investigate the nighttime sky from their school or community. This inquiry might involve measurements of sky brightness, observations of celestial objects, and analysis of how artificial lighting affects what people can see. While the activity is not itself an investigation of extraterrestrial life, it can introduce the technologies, observational constraints, and social conditions through which humans study the universe.

In each case, place serves as more than an instructional setting. It becomes a source of questions and evidence, a context for interpreting knowledge, and a means of examining the relationships between science and human experience.
